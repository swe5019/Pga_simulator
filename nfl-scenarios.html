NFL Game Stack Scenarios

A tournament is not won by nine players each having a nice day. It is won when one game goes off and you own several pieces of it, because players in the same game rise together. So this does not rank players. It plays the slate out thousands of times, solves the lineup that would have won each of those slates, and then asks what those winners had in common. Almost always the answer is a game rather than a player. Deep is how many of your nine come from that one game.

Cheap correlation is the piece of that game nobody is taking. A lineup that stacks a game has to choose which players to stack it with, and the obvious ones are obvious to everybody. The column shows whoever carries the most ceiling per point of ownership, among players with a real enough role to be worth it: he moves with the game almost as much as the popular piece, at a fraction of the ownership, and the salary he saves is what pays for somebody expensive elsewhere. Ownership is never divided by less than one percent, because the ownership model carries about four points of error and the difference between a tenth of a percent and eight tenths is noise rather than an edge. The lineup that won the 1 November Millionaire did exactly this, taking a $2,500 tight end owned by 3% of the field as the fourth piece of its stack rather than the $5,800 running back everyone else used.

Half of this is measured and half is a model, and the halves are not equally good. The Wins% column is measured: it is the share of simulated slates whose best possible lineup had that shape, and nothing about the field goes into it. The Field% column is a simulation of what everybody else builds, and it rests on projected ownership that has been checked against one real contest and came back over-dispersed, with 4.15 points of average error. Leverage divides one by the other, so it is only as good as the weaker half. Use it to rank games against each other. Do not read 3.0× as "three times the edge".

How this is built

It keeps the joint structure instead of averaging it away

The projection build already simulates the whole slate thousands of times with team mates correlated, so that a quarterback and his receiver rise and fall together. It then throws that structure away and reports an average for each player, which is what every projection source does.

This page keeps it. For each simulated slate it solves for the lineup that would have won that particular world, then groups those winners by where their points came from. A game only needs a fat enough right tail to show up here. It does not need a high projected total, which is exactly why a board ranked by mean projection cannot see it.

Why the field column is the weak half

To say that nobody else is on a game, you have to know what everybody else is doing. That means simulating a field, and a simulated field is only as good as the ownership it is built from. Ownership here is a model rather than a measurement, and when it was finally checked against a real contest it was systematically over-dispersed: the players it called heavy chalk came in at less than half its number, and the ones it buried came in at nearly three times.

The field is also built to stack the way real entries do, with a quarterback deliberately paired with his own receivers and often somebody from the other side. That matters more than it sounds: a field that only stacks by accident makes deep stacks look rare and precious when they are neither. The rates it stacks at are reasonable assumptions, not measurements, and until they are measured from a real contest the shape ranking should be read as provisional.

What to do with it

Read the win column as which games have the fattest tails on this slate, and the leverage column as a rough ordering of which of those the field is least likely to be crowding. Neither column tells you which game will actually go off. Nothing can. What it does tell you is where the payoff is concentrated if one of them does, which is the part that is knowable before kickoff.
