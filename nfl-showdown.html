NFL Showdown Scenarios

A showdown is six players from one game, one of them a captain who scores and costs one and a half times. Because every lineup is in the same game, asking which game to stack is meaningless here. The questions that actually separate two lineups are who captains, how the six split across the two sides, and what kind of game it turns into. So that is what this measures: it plays the game out thousands of times with players correlated, solves the lineup that would have won each one, and groups those winners.

Wins% and Field% are two different things, shown side by side and never divided. Wins% is measured from the simulation: the share of games whose best possible lineup captained that player. Field% is the modelled share of entries that captain him, which is a separate thing you are not told before lock. Captain ownership sums to 100% across the slate while flex ownership sums to 500%, so one popular captain swallows an enormous part of it. There is still no flex ownership here, and so no whole-lineup leverage number: only the captain slot has a measured denominator. Dividing one column into the other is specifically a mistake. On the two contests where it could be checked it ranked the actual winning captain 12th of 21 and 20th of 23, against 1st and 10th for the win rate on its own.

Game script

With one game you cannot ask which game goes off, so you ask how this one goes off. Each simulated game is bucketed by how much the two sides produced and how far apart they finished, using this game's own distribution rather than fixed point totals, and the winning shapes are reported inside each bucket. This is the part that should change what you build.

How this is built

The solve is exact, which matters more here than on a main slate

A single-game pool is about two dozen players once the unprojectable deep roster is set aside, and a lineup is six slots. That is small enough to solve properly: every player is tried as captain, and every legal way of filling the other five slots is scored against the cap. Nothing is left to a greedy search.

That is not a detail. A captain is decided by a single point of score, so an approximate fill would hand the slot to the wrong player while still producing a plausible-looking table. The solver is checked against an independently written brute force over random games and agrees to within a rounding error, and it is separately checked to never break the salary cap, never duplicate the captain into a flex slot, and never return a lineup missing one of the two teams.

Why a showdown suits this method better than a main slate

On a twelve-game slate, saying a construction is uncrowded requires modelling what several hundred thousand entries look like across nearly six hundred players, and that model is only as good as its ownership projections. Here the pool is the game. All the correlation that matters is inside it, which is exactly what the simulation models best, and there are no cross-game independence assumptions left to get wrong.

What it does not tell you

It does not say which way the game will go. Nothing can. It says which six-man constructions win if the game breaks a particular way, and how often each way happens in the simulation, which is the part that is knowable before kickoff. It also does not yet say how many other people built the same thing, and that gap is stated on the page rather than papered over with a number.
